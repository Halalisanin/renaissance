Renaissance of the Poor Soul

On the Restless and Weary Soul

The Spiritual Meaning of Waiting

Halalisani Ngema February 2026

Originally published on renaissance.liviyo.co.za

There is a man in the Bible who waited thirty-eight years. He lay beside a pool in Jerusalem, hoping that one day the water would move, and when it did, someone would help him in before anyone else got there. Thirty-eight years. Think about that. Most people cannot wait thirty-eight minutes for a WhatsApp reply without spiralling. This man waited thirty-eight years for his body to work. And when Jesus finally found him, the question was not "Do you want to be healed?" It was "Do you want to be made well?" There is a difference, and it changed the way I understand waiting forever.

I used to think waiting was just the space between events. The nothing that happens before the something. A hallway you walk through on the way to the room where your life actually begins. But I was wrong. Waiting is not the hallway. Waiting is the room.

When Waiting Becomes Identity

There comes a point in prolonged waiting where you no longer feel like a person who is waiting. You feel like a person who is waiting. The waiting stops being something you do and becomes something you are. It attaches itself to your identity the way a vine attaches itself to a wall — slowly, invisibly, and then one day you realise the wall is covered.

I lived in this kind of waiting for years. Waiting for the business to take off. Waiting for the relationship to stabilise. Waiting for the money to come. Waiting for the grief to pass. Waiting for God to answer the prayers that felt like they were hitting the ceiling and falling back down. And somewhere in the middle of all that waiting, I stopped being a man who was going through a hard time and became a man whose hard time had become him.

The man at the pool of Bethesda understood this. When Jesus asked him if he wanted to be made well, he did not say yes. He said, "Sir, I have no one to help me into the pool when the water is stirred." He did not answer the question. He explained his situation. Because when you have waited long enough, your situation becomes your explanation. It becomes how you introduce yourself. It becomes the first thing you tell people, even if they did not ask.

Passive Waiting vs. Active Waiting

Not all waiting is the same. Some waiting is passive — you have done everything you can, and now you are simply in the space between action and result. You have submitted the application. You have made the call. You have planted the seed. And now you wait. This kind of waiting is uncomfortable but honest. It is the waiting of a farmer who has done the work and now trusts the soil.

But there is another kind of waiting. The kind that has lost its object. The kind where you are no longer waiting for something — you are just waiting. This is the waiting that has become a way of life. It is the waiting of the restless soul, the one who cannot settle because something in them knows that where they are is not where they are meant to be, but they have no idea where that is.

In the book, I write about the restless soul as someone who moves constantly but never arrives. They change jobs, change cities, change churches, change partners — not because they are flighty, but because they are searching. There is a hunger in them that nothing seems to satisfy. And the waiting that comes with this restlessness is not passive at all. It is active, but directionless. It is the spiritual equivalent of walking fast in the wrong direction.

Then there is the weary soul. The weary soul is different. The weary soul has stopped moving. Not because they have found what they were looking for, but because they are too tired to look anymore. The weary soul sits down. The weary soul says, "I have been waiting for thirty-eight years, and the water has never moved for me." The weary soul is not restless — it is exhausted. And the waiting it does is the heaviest kind of all: the waiting that has given up on itself.

"There is a waiting that looks like faith from the outside but feels like abandonment from the inside. And the hardest part is that no one can tell the difference. Not even you."

The Waiting That God Enters

Here is what strikes me about the story at the pool: Jesus walked past everyone else and went to the man who had been waiting the longest. Not the sickest. Not the most religious. The most patient. The one who had been there the longest. The one whose waiting had become so thorough that it had calcified into his identity.

I think about this a lot. Because it suggests something about the nature of God that we do not often talk about. It suggests that God is attracted to endurance. Not success. Not performance. Endurance. The kind that does not make a good Instagram post. The kind that is just one foot in front of the other, day after day, with no evidence that it matters.

In the book, I argue that the pool of Bethesda is a metaphor for every place where we wait for something to change — every hospital bed, every empty bank account, every silent phone, every unanswered prayer. And I argue that Jesus's question — "Do you want to be made well?" — is not as simple as it sounds. Because being made well means your waiting is over. And when your waiting is over, you have to figure out who you are without it.

That is the part no one talks about. The man who waited thirty-eight years at the pool did not just receive healing that day. He lost his identity. He was no longer the man who waited at the pool. He had to become someone new. And becoming someone new, after decades of being someone old, is its own kind of terrifying.

What I Learned in My Waiting

I will not pretend I handled my seasons of waiting well. I did not. I was impatient, angry, sometimes faithless. I yelled at God more times than I can count. I made decisions in my restlessness that I regret. I sat in the weariness and let it tell me lies about myself — that I was forgotten, that I was being punished, that I had done something so wrong that God had closed the door permanently.

But I also learned something that I could not have learned any other way. I learned that waiting, when it is done honestly — when you do not pretend it is not happening, when you do not numb yourself or perform your way through it — waiting has a gift. It is a terrible gift. A gift wrapped in sandpaper. But it is a gift nonetheless.

The gift is this: waiting strips away everything that is not essential. It takes your ambition and your plans and your carefully constructed self-image and it holds them up to the light and says, "Is this really you? Or is this what you thought you were supposed to be?" And when the dust settles, what remains — if you are honest, if you are willing — is something smaller than what you started with, but truer. Something that cannot be taken from you because it is not made of performance or position or other people's approval.

It is you. Just you. Sitting by a pool. Still waiting. Still here. And that, I have come to believe, is not a failure of faith.

It is faith in its most honest form.
