What Is the Renaissance of the Poor Soul?

Halalisani Ngema January 2026

Originally published on renaissance.liviyo.co.za

There is a word people use when they talk about poverty that has nothing to do with money. It is a quiet word. A word that sits in the chest, not the wallet. And when I first began writing this book, I did not know that this word would become its centre — its heartbeat, even. That word is soul. And the poverty I was trying to describe was never going to be solved by a bank statement improving.

The phrase "poor soul" rolls off the tongue like a sigh. You have heard people say it. Maybe you have said it yourself — standing over a pot of boiling water at five in the morning, or sitting in a hospital corridor waiting for news that will change everything. "That poor soul." You say it about someone else, but somewhere in the saying, you recognise yourself.

A poor soul is not someone who lacks ambition. A poor soul is not someone who did not try hard enough. A poor soul is a person who has been emptied — slowly, quietly, sometimes over years — by life itself. By loss. By disappointment. By the relentless accumulation of moments where hope was offered and then withdrawn. A poor soul is someone who still believes in God, perhaps, but whose belief has become a bruise rather than a balm.

That is who I wrote this book for.

The Five Soul Types

As I sat with my own experiences — the failures, the grief, the long seasons of waiting — I began to notice that not all spiritual pain feels the same. There are different textures to suffering. Different weights. And the way out of one kind of darkness is not always the way out of another. So I started mapping what I had lived, and five soul types emerged. Not as a theory. As a testimony.

The Poor Soul

The poor soul is depleted. Emptied by prolonged hardship — financial, relational, spiritual. This is the person who has given and given and has nothing left. They are not lazy. They are spent. Their poverty is not of effort but of resource. They have been running on fumes for so long they have forgotten what a full tank feels like.

The Afflicted Soul

The afflicted soul carries pain that feels like it was assigned. There is a sense of being targeted — by circumstance, by illness, by a pattern of suffering that makes no logical sense. The afflicted soul wrestles not just with pain but with the question of why. Why me? Why this? Why now?

The Lost Soul

The lost soul does not know where they are going anymore. They may once have had direction — a calling, a plan, a sense of purpose. But something shifted. The path disappeared. And now they are walking without a map, without a compass, sometimes without the energy to care which way they face.

The Restless Soul

The restless soul cannot settle. They move from job to job, relationship to relationship, city to city, church to church — not because they want novelty, but because they are searching for something they cannot name. There is a hunger in them that nothing seems to feed. A stirring that will not go quiet.

The Weary Soul

The weary soul is tired. Not sleepy — tired. Bone-deep, soul-deep exhaustion that sleep does not fix. This is the soul that has endured too much, too long, with too little help. They are not weak. They have simply been strong for too long without rest.

And there is one more, though I hesitated to name it because it is the hardest one to admit to:

The Tormented Soul

The tormented soul is the one who suffers not just from circumstance but from shame. From the stories they tell themselves about what their failures mean. This soul does not only feel pain — it feels accused. It carries guilt like a second skeleton, holding it up even when it wants to collapse.

I did not write these types to categorise people like specimens. I wrote them because when I finally saw my own soul clearly — not as a vague cloud of sadness but as a specific condition with a specific name — something shifted. Not dramatically. Not like the movies. But the way dawn shifts: slowly, then all at once, you realise you can see again.

This Is Not a Self-Help Book

I need to say this clearly because I know what it looks like from the outside. A book about the soul, with a word like "renaissance" in the title — it must be another one of those books that tells you to wake up at 4am and journal your way to happiness. It is not that. I have nothing against early mornings or journals. But this book was not written from a place of instruction. It was written from a place of wreckage.

I wrote most of it in the hardest season of my life. After the scam that left me stranded at Park Station with nothing. After my mother died. After the drinking got worse before it got better. After the business failures stacked up so high I could not see over them. I wrote it in hospital waiting rooms and on long walks through Johannesburg when I could not afford the taxi. I wrote it with shaking hands and honest prayers — prayers that were more like arguments with God than conversations.

"I am not writing to you from the mountaintop. I am writing to you from the valley. And I am telling you — the valley has things to teach you that the mountaintop never will."

The renaissance — the renewal, the coming back to life — is not a guarantee. It is not a formula. It is a possibility that presents itself when a person is willing to look at their soul honestly and say: I know what I have become. And I am not staying here.

A Framework for the Reader

The book moves through these soul types not as abstract categories but as lived chapters. Each one is rooted in scripture — the Psalms, primarily, because David knew what it meant to be every single one of these souls — and each one is grounded in my own experience. I do not quote theologians. I quote the man at the pool of Bethesda who waited thirty-eight years. I quote the woman who touched the hem of Jesus's garment. I quote my own journals, my own desperate prayers, my own small acts of faith when faith felt like a language I had forgotten.

The framework is not meant to be diagnostic in the clinical sense. You are not meant to read it and say, "Ah, I am a restless soul, so chapters seven through nine apply to me." It does not work like that. The soul does not obey neat categories. You will recognise yourself in all of them, at different seasons, on different days. The framework exists to give you language — to help you name what you have been carrying without a name.

Because naming is the first step. It always has been. Even God asked Adam, "Where are you?" Not because God did not know. But because Adam needed to say it.

This Book Was Written for You

I do not know your story. I do not know what brought you to this page — whether it was a search engine, a recommendation, or the kind of late-night scrolling that happens when sleep will not come and the weight of everything presses down. But I know this: if you are here, something in the phrase "poor soul" spoke to you. Something in you recognised it.

This book was written for you. Not for the version of you that has it together. Not for the version that posts inspirational quotes on Monday mornings. For the real you. The one who is tired. The one who wonders if God has forgotten. The one who is not sure they believe anymore but cannot quite let go. The one who has been knocked down so many times that getting up feels less like courage and more like reflex.

You are not alone. You are not forgotten. And the fact that you are still here — reading, searching, hoping against hope — tells me something about your soul that your soul may have forgotten.

It is still alive.
