Renaissance of the Poor Soul

On Endurance

How to Heal From Repeated Failure

Halalisani Ngema February 2026

Originally published on renaissance.liviyo.co.za

There is a difference between failing once and becoming a person to whom failure happens. Most people do not understand this distinction until they have lived it. And once you have lived it – once you have watched a job disappear, a relationship collapse, a savings account drain to zero, and then watched it happen again, and again – you begin to understand that repeated failure does not just take things from you. It takes something from inside you. It rewrites your story. It changes the way you walk into a room, the way you answer the phone, the way you pray.

I know this because I have lived it. Not once. Not as a single dramatic event that makes a good story at dinner. But as a pattern – a season that stretched so long I stopped believing it was a season and started believing it was my permanent address.

The Difference Between a Failure and a Pattern

A single failure – a business that does not work out, a relationship that ends – is painful but navigable. You grieve. You learn. You recover. You carry the scar, but the scar fades. You can tell the story with a bit of distance, maybe even with some humour.

A pattern is different. A pattern is when the same type of failure keeps appearing in your life like a recurring character in a story you did not choose to write. Job loss after job loss. Relationship breakdown after relationship breakdown. Financial crisis after financial crisis. And somewhere in the middle of it all, a voice starts whispering – not the voice of the devil, not necessarily, but the voice of exhaustion – saying: maybe this is just who you are.

That voice is the most dangerous thing about repeated failure. Not the loss of money. Not the loss of position. The loss of a sense of self. The slow erosion of the belief that you are someone to whom good things can happen.

I remember sitting in my car – I think it was the second car I lost – and realising that I could not remember the last time I had expected something to go well. Not hoped. Not wished. Expected. The expectation itself had been beaten out of me, not by any single blow, but by the accumulation of blows. I was still standing, technically. But I was standing the way a fence post stands – upright, but only because it has not yet been pushed over.

What the Soul Accumulates

When failure is repeated, the soul does something that the mind does not always register. It accumulates wounds the way a wall accumulates layers of paint – each one thin, almost invisible, but together creating a thickness that changes the shape of the thing. The soul becomes heavy. Not with sin, not with laziness, not with any of the accusations that people casually throw at those who are struggling. It becomes heavy with experience. With the sheer weight of having endured too much, too often, with too little support.

This is what I mean in the book when I write about the poor soul. The poverty is not of effort. The person who has failed repeatedly has often tried harder than anyone in the room. The poverty is of spirit. It is the emptiness that comes from pouring out again and again with no one pouring back in.

In the book, I describe this accumulation using the image of a vessel that has been cracked and repaired so many times that the cracks themselves have become the structure. You are held together not by your original wholeness but by your history of breaking. And there is something in that – something honest, something even sacred – that we do not talk about enough.

Endurance as Spiritual Practice

We do not like the word endurance. It sounds passive. It sounds like gritting your teeth and waiting for the storm to pass, which is exactly what it is, but we have made that sound like a lesser form of faith. We prefer words like breakthrough, victory, promotion, breakthrough. We prefer the language of arrival.

But what about the people who are not arriving? What about the people who are still on the road, still walking, still carrying the same weight they carried yesterday? What does faith look like for them?

In the book, I write this:

"Endurance, done with awareness, is one of the most powerful spiritual practices available to the human soul."

I mean that. I did not write it for effect. I wrote it because I discovered it in the worst moment of my life – sitting at Park Station in Johannesburg, with nothing, after the scam that took everything. I had no money. No plan. No one expecting me anywhere. And in that moment, I did something that surprised me: I stayed. I did not run. I did not numb myself. I did not pretend it was not happening. I stayed with it. I endured it. And in the staying, something happened that I did not expect.

I found God.

Not in the way you find a wallet you lost. In the way you find silence when you stop talking. In the way you find the ground when you finally stop trying to fly. God was in the endurance itself. Not in the breakthrough that came after. In the endurance.

The Difference Between Passive and Active Endurance

I want to be clear because I do not want this to sound like resignation. There is a difference between enduring and giving up. There is a difference between sitting in the fire and lying down in it.

Passive endurance is when you stop trying altogether. When you let failure define you so completely that you stop showing up. You stop applying for jobs. You stop returning calls. You stop praying. You stop caring. That is not endurance. That is surrender, and it is not the kind that leads to life.

Active endurance is different. It is when you continue to show up even though you have every reason not to. It is when you apply for the job even though the last five did not work out. It is when you make the phone call even though the last ten went unanswered. It is when you pray even though your prayers feel like they are bouncing off the ceiling. Active endurance is not optimistic. It is faithful. It does not promise that things will improve. It simply refuses to leave the room.

I think this is what the Psalms are about when they say, "I am worn out waiting for God to act." That is not a failure of faith. That is the most honest form of faith there is. It is faith that has been stripped of pretence, stripped of performance, stripped of the need to appear strong. It is faith with nothing left but itself.

What Healing Actually Looks Like

I will not pretend that healing from repeated failure is quick or linear. It is not. Some days you will feel like you have turned a corner. Other days you will be back in the thick of it, wondering if anything has changed at all. That is normal. That is the shape of real healing – not a straight line upward but a spiral that slowly, slowly widens.

But there are things I have learned. Things I include in the book. Not as a prescription – I am not qualified to prescribe anything – but as a testimony. What helped me. What I saw. What I held onto when there was almost nothing left to hold.

Naming your failure honestly – not minimising it, not exaggerating it, but saying, "This happened, and it hurt, and I am still here." Finding at least one person who will not try to fix you but will sit with you in it. Doing the smallest thing you can do each day – not because it will solve the problem, but because it keeps the muscle of participation alive. And praying, even if the prayer is only: "I am still here. Are you?"

These are not strategies. They are small acts of faithfulness. And small acts of faithfulness, repeated over time, are how the soul heals. Not all at once. Not dramatically. But in the way that soil recovers after a drought – slowly, quietly, with a patience that is not passive but deeply, fiercely alive.

You are not your failures. You are the soul that has endured them. And that soul – your soul – is more resilient than you know.
