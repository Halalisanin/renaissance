The Difference Between a Lost Soul and a Poor Soul

August 22, 2026 · By Halalisani Ngema

When I was younger, I thought I was a lost soul. I said it about myself – "I am lost." It sounded deep. It sounded like the kind of thing a person says when they have been through real things. But years later, sitting with the wreckage of my life, I realized I had been using the wrong word. I was not lost. I was poor. There is a difference, and it changed everything when I understood it.

In the book, I identify six soul types. Six ways the human spirit can be in distress. Each one is real. Each one is valid. But they are not the same thing, and confusing them can keep you stuck in the wrong kind of healing.

The Poor Soul

The poor soul is the one the book is named after. Not because it is the most dramatic or the most tragic, but because it is the most overlooked. The poor soul is the soul that has been emptied. Not by choice. By life. By loss. By the slow accumulation of days where you gave and gave and gave until there was nothing left to give.

The poor soul knows it is poor. That is the defining feature. It looks at itself honestly and says, "I have nothing. I am empty. I do not have the spiritual resources to get through this week." There is no pretence. There is no performance. The poor soul is the most honest soul there is, because it has lost the energy to lie about its condition.

And here is the thing the church does not always understand: the poor soul is not a failure. It is a condition that is wide open to grace. Poverty of spirit – Jesus said, "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven." Not "blessed are the strong." Not "blessed are the ones who have it together." Blessed are the poor. The ones who know they are empty. Because only the empty vessel can be filled.

The Lost Soul

The lost soul is different. The lost soul does not know it is lost. That is the cruel part. It wanders. It drifts. It fills its life with noise, with activity, with substances, with relationships, with anything that distracts from the fundamental disorientation of not knowing where it belongs.

I have known lost souls. Perhaps you have too. They are not necessarily suffering in obvious ways. They might have jobs. They might have families. They might appear, from the outside, to be doing fine. But there is a hollowness in their eyes when they are alone. A restlessness they cannot name. They are like a person walking through a city they have never visited, looking at street signs they cannot read, pretending they know where they are going.

The lost soul is cut off from belonging. Not just from God – from themselves. From their own story. They do not know who they are because they have been running from the question for so long that the running has become the identity.

"The poor soul knows it is poor. The lost soul does not yet know it is lost. That is the difference between a wound and an infection – the wound announces itself. The infection hides."

Why the Distinction Matters

If you treat a lost soul like a poor soul, you will tell them to be still and know that God is God. But they do not need stillness – they need a mirror. They need someone to say, "You are not where you think you are. You have been running for years, and you are not free. You are lost."

If you treat a poor soul like a lost soul, you will send them on a journey of self-discovery when what they actually need is rest. They do not need to find themselves. They need to be found. They need someone to sit with them in the emptiness and say, "You are not alone. Your poverty is not your identity. It is your doorway."

I wrote this book for the poor soul because I am one. I know the terrain. I know what it feels like to have nothing left – no faith, no strength, no answers – and to still somehow believe that God has not abandoned you. That is not stupidity. That is not weakness. That is the poorest, purest kind of faith there is.

The Other Souls

The book describes six types, and I will not give them all away here. But briefly: there is the broken soul, shattered by trauma. The restless soul, always searching. The weary soul, exhausted by the fight. The afflicted soul, crushed by suffering. And the returning soul, the one making its way back.

Each one has its own pain. Each one has its own path. And the reason I wrote about all six is because I believe that spiritual life is not one-size-fits-all. The church often speaks as if there is one way to be spiritual, one way to suffer well, one way to pray. But a mother in Soweto who has buried a child does not have the same spiritual needs as a young man in Cape Town who has never been to church.

We need language for different kinds of suffering. We need permission to say, "This is what I am going through, and it does not match the sermon on Sunday." Because when your experience does not match the teaching, you do not throw away your experience. You look for a better teaching.

You Might Be a Poor Soul

If you are reading this and something in you whispers, "That is me – I am the poor one, the empty one," then hear me: you are not broken. You are not failing. You are in a condition that is ready for grace. The poor soul is the soul God fills. Not because it earned it. Because it is open.

You do not need to fix yourself before God meets you. You do not need to get your theology right, your prayer life right, your behaviour right. You need to be honest. And the poor soul is already honest, because it has nothing left to pretend with.

That is not a weakness. That is a superpower.

The door is open. It has always been open. The question is not whether you are worthy of walking through it. The question is whether you will let yourself be found.
