The Power of Returning (Teshuvah)

September 4, 2026 · By Halalisani Ngema

There is a Hebrew word that changed how I understand everything – not just religion, not just theology, but life itself. The word is teshuvah. It is usually translated as "repentance," but that English word has been so beaten up by centuries of fire-and-brimstone preaching that it no longer carries what the Hebrew actually means. Teshuvah does not mean "I am sorry." It means "I am turning around."

That difference – between apology and reorientation – is the difference between a bandage and a healing.

What Teshuvah Actually Is

In Jewish tradition, teshuvah is not a single act. It is a process. You acknowledge where you have gone wrong. You feel genuine remorse – not just guilt, which is self-focused, but sorrow, which is other-focused. You make restitution where you can. And then you change direction. You walk the other way. Not perfectly. Not completely. But you turn.

The rabbis teach that teshuvah is always available. There is no sin so great that the door of return is closed. There is no distance so vast that God cannot reach you there. This is not cheap grace – it does not say that actions have no consequences. It says something more radical: that no matter what you have done, you are not defined by it. You can turn. You can come back. The path home exists even when you cannot see it.

I needed to hear that. Not as a theological concept. As a lifeline.

The Restless Soul

In the book, I write about the restless soul – one of the six types. The restless soul is the one that cannot stay. It runs. It moves from relationship to relationship, from job to job, from city to city, from ideology to ideology, always searching for something it cannot name. It is the soul that Augustine described when he wrote, "Our hearts are restless until they rest in You."

The restless soul does not need more movement. It needs return. It needs to stop running long enough to remember where it came from. Not a place. Not a church. A direction. A home that is not geographical but spiritual – the place inside you where God dwells, where your truest self lives, where the noise finally stops and you can hear the voice that has been calling you all along.

Teshuvah is the restless soul's antidote. Not because it ends the restlessness with a command, but because it redirects it. The energy you have been spending on running – what if you turned it around and spent it on returning? What if the same intensity that kept you fleeing became the intensity that brought you home?

"Teshuvah is not the end of the journey. It is the moment you realize you were walking in the wrong direction and you have the courage to turn."

Every Tradition Has a Word for It

One of the things I explore in the book is how every major spiritual tradition has this concept. Not just Judaism. Not just Christianity. Every tradition that takes the human soul seriously has a version of return.

In Islam, there is tawbah – which also means turning, repenting, returning to God. The Prophet Muhammad is reported to have said that God is more joyful over the repentance of His servant than a man who finds his lost camel in the desert after having given up hope. That image – a man in the desert, hopeless, and then finding what he thought was gone forever – that is tawbah. That is return.

In Christianity, we have repentance – from the Greek metanoia, which means a change of mind, a transformation of the inner self. Not just feeling sorry but thinking differently. Seeing differently. Living differently. The prodigal son is the great Christian story of teshuvah: a young man who took everything, wasted everything, ended up feeding pigs, and then – in a moment of clarity – decided to go home. Not because he deserved to be welcomed back. Because home was still home.

And in Africa – in my own tradition, in the traditions of my ancestors – there is ubuntu reintegration. In many African cultures, when a person has strayed, there is a process of bringing them back into the community. Not by ignoring what happened. Not by pretending the offence did not occur. But by acknowledging it, dealing with it, and then welcoming the person back into the circle. Because in ubuntu, a person is a person through other people. To be cut off from the community is to be cut off from yourself. And return – teshuvah, metanoia, reintegration – is the way back to being fully human.

Why We Resist Return

If return is always possible, why do so many of us refuse it? I think there are three reasons, and I have lived all three.

The first is shame. Shame says, "You have gone too far. You have done too much. There is no way back." Shame is the voice that tells you that your past defines your future. That you are your worst moment. That the damage you have caused is permanent and unforgivable. Shame is a liar. A powerful one, but a liar.

The second is pride. Pride says, "You do not need to go back. You were right to leave. The people you left behind were wrong. Going back would mean admitting defeat." Pride keeps the restless soul restless. It keeps the lost soul lost. And it dresses itself up as strength when it is actually fear – fear of vulnerability, fear of being seen, fear of the humility that return requires.

The third is fear of the unknown. We know who we are when we are running. We have built an identity around the running. Going home means becoming someone we have forgotten how to be. And the unknown – even when it is home – is terrifying.

The Door Is Never Closed

I want to end with this, and I want you to hear it not as a preacher speaking but as a man who has stood at the door and wondered if it would open: the door is never closed.

Never.

I do not care what you have done. I do not care how far you have gone. I do not care how many years you have spent running. The door is still open. It has always been open. You are the one who has been standing outside, telling yourself it is locked.

Teshuvah is the act of walking toward the door. Not running – you have done enough running. Walking. One step. Then another. The door does not require you to be perfect. It does not require you to have all the answers. It requires you to turn.

The restless soul – the one who has wandered, who has searched, who has filled every silence with noise – that soul is being called home. Right now. Not tomorrow. Not when you are ready. Now. Because teshuvah does not wait for readiness. It creates it.

You do not need to earn your way back. You do not need to deserve it. You just need to turn around.

The door is open. It has always been open. Come home.
