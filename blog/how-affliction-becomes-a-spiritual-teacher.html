How Affliction Can Become a Spiritual Teacher

August 29, 2026 · By Halalisani Ngema

The word "affliction" comes from the Latin afflictio — to strike, to crush. Not to nudge. Not to challenge. To crush. There is no softness in this word, no gentle metaphor. When affliction comes, it comes like a hammer. And the first thing it breaks is your pretence.

I want to be careful here. I am not about to tell you that your suffering is beautiful. I am not about to say God gave it to you for a reason. I have sat with people who have lost children, who have buried husbands, who have watched their homes burn — and I will never say to them, "This is a gift." That is a cruelty dressed in religious clothing.

What I will say is this: affliction, once it has done its worst, can become a teacher. Not because it is good. But because it is honest. And honesty, even brutal honesty, is the beginning of all real spiritual growth.

What Comfort Conceals

When life is going well — when there is food in the fridge, money in the account, health in the body — we build a self. We build a version of ourselves that is competent, capable, in control. We pray our prayers and read our books and attend our services and we believe, sincerely believe, that we are spiritual people.

Then affliction comes. And it strips all of that away.

I watched it happen in my own life. When the money dried up, when the relationships collapsed, when the body began to fail — I discovered that most of what I thought was faith was actually comfort. I had faith when things were going well. But when things fell apart, the faith fell apart with them. And that told me something devastating: I had built my spiritual life on a foundation that only worked when the weather was good.

Affliction exposed that. Brutally. Publicly. In front of my family, my community, my own mirror.

"Comfort is not the enemy. But comfort is a liar. It tells you that you are strong when you have simply been lucky."

The Education No One Asks For

In the book, I write about the afflicted soul — one of the six types I describe. The afflicted soul is the one that has been struck down. Not by a single event, though that can happen. More often, it is a slow crushing. The accumulation of years of pain. Poverty that does not lift. Illness that does not heal. Grief that does not pass.

And in the midst of that crushing, something begins to happen that no one expects. The afflicted soul starts to see things it could not see before. When you are comfortable, you can afford to ignore the uncomfortable truths. You can ignore your own selfishness. You can ignore the gap between what you say you believe and how you actually live. You can ignore the people you have hurt, the promises you have broken, the ways you have been complicit in systems that harm others.

Affliction removes the luxury of ignorance. When everything has been taken from you, you can no longer hide from yourself. You are left with the raw, unedited version of who you are. And that version — that is where the spiritual life actually begins.

Not Punishment. Education.

I know the theology that says suffering is punishment. I grew up hearing it. "God is teaching you a lesson." "God gives His hardest battles to His strongest soldiers." I understand why people say these things. They are trying to make meaning out of pain. And meaning-making is a human survival mechanism — we need to believe that our suffering is not random, not pointless, not just the universe grinding us to dust.

But I have come to believe something different. Suffering is not punishment. Affliction is not God's way of paying you back for your sins. If God wanted to punish you, He would not need to use suffering — He is God. He has other tools. Affliction is something else. It is, in the brutal and beautiful language of the mystics, a school. The hardest school. The one where the fees are paid in tears and the curriculum is written in scars.

What does affliction teach? It teaches you what you actually believe, not what you say you believe. It teaches you who actually loves you, and who was only there for the good times. It teaches you the difference between strength and the performance of strength. It teaches you that you are not as in control as you thought — and that this is not a tragedy but a liberation.

The Difference Between Two Kinds of Affliction

Not all affliction transforms. Some affliction destroys. I need to say this clearly, because I do not want anyone reading this book or this blog to think I am romanticising suffering.

Affliction that destroys is affliction that isolates. It is the suffering that cuts you off from community, from hope, from the possibility that things could be different. It is the kind of pain that makes you bitter, that hardens your heart, that turns you against yourself and against others. That is affliction as a prison. And it is real. And I have seen it destroy people I love.

Affliction that transforms is affliction that breaks you open instead of breaking you down. It is the suffering that humbles you, that makes you teachable, that drives you to your knees not in defeat but in surrender. It is the kind of pain that strips away the false self and reveals the true self underneath. That is affliction as a teacher. And it requires one thing that the destroying kind does not: it requires that you do not give up.

I am not saying it is easy. I am not saying you should pretend to be okay when you are not. I am saying that if you can hold on — even by your fingertips — affliction can become the most honest education you have ever received.

The Afflicted Soul Is Not Alone

One of the things I have learned is that affliction lies. It tells you that you are the only one. That nobody understands. That God has abandoned you. That your pain is unique and therefore unshareable. This is a lie. The most dangerous lie affliction tells.

You are not alone. Your suffering is not unique in the way that matters — not unique in the sense that no one else has felt it. Others have been crushed. Others have been struck down. And some of them — not all, but some — have come through the other side with a depth, a compassion, a wisdom that could not have been acquired any other way.

I am one of them. I do not say this with pride. I say it with wonder. Because there were days when I was certain that affliction had destroyed me. And yet here I am, writing this, testifying that the teacher did its work. Not gently. Not fairly. But thoroughly.

The poor soul — the afflicted soul — is not the end of the story. It is the beginning of a deeper one.
