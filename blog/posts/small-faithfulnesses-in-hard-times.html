Small Faithfulnesses in Hard Times

August 15, 2026 · By Halalisani Ngema

I stopped praying for an hour. I stopped reading the whole Bible in a year. I stopped making promises to God that I knew by Thursday I would have broken. And when I stopped, something strange happened – faith came closer, not further away.

There is a lie the church tells us, though it does not know it is lying. The lie is this: faith is supposed to look like a certain thing. Kneeling. Fasting. An hour of intercession. A journal full of scriptures. And when you cannot do that – when life has beaten you so low that the best you can manage is a whisper between sobs – you begin to believe you have failed God. That your faith is small. That you are small.

But I want to tell you something that the book taught me, something that changed how I understand every single day of my survival: the small thing IS the faith. When you cannot do the big thing, the small thing becomes everything.

The Verse You Can Barely Hold

There was a season – I write about it in the chapter on anchoring yourself in small faithfulnesses – where I could not read my Bible. Not because I did not want to. Because every time I opened it, the words blurred. Grief does that. Poverty does that. The weight of raising children in a house where the roof leaks and the future is uncertain does that.

But one verse stayed. Psalm 27:10. "Though my father and mother forsake me, the Lord will receive me." I did not read it every day. Sometimes I only remembered it at two in the morning when the ceiling creaked and I was afraid. But that one verse – that single sentence – became the thread I held onto. Not a rope. Not a chain. A thread.

And a thread, when it is the only thing between you and the abyss, is enough.

One Breath Is a Prayer

The mystics knew this. The Desert Fathers knew this. In the Orthodox tradition, there is the Jesus Prayer – "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me." Just that. Over and over. Not because the words are magical but because the repetition is an act of turning toward God. Even when your mind is foggy. Even when you are not sure God is listening.

I learned to pray with my breath. Not metaphors. Literal breath. In – "Lord." Out – "help me." That was the prayer. Some mornings, that was the only prayer. And I had to learn to stop despising it. I had to learn that God was not standing at the door of heaven with a stopwatch, measuring whether my devotion was adequate.

God does not measure devotion. God receives it. Whatever you bring – a sermon or a sigh – He receives it.

Survival IS a Spiritual Act

This is the part that broke me open. We think spiritual acts are the ones that happen in church, on our knees, with worship music playing. But what about the mother who gets up at four in the morning to catch a taxi so her children can eat – is that not a spiritual act? What about the man who chooses not to drink his pain away on a Friday night, who sits with the emptiness instead – is that not prayer?

In the book, I write that survival faithfulness is the most honest faith there is. It has no performance. It has no audience. It is just you and the quiet decision to keep going, to keep trusting, even when trust feels like a word you once knew the meaning of but have now forgotten.

Getting out of bed when depression tells you to stay down – that is worship. Choosing kindness when you have been treated cruelly – that is intercession. Feeding your children when you are not sure where next week's money is coming from – that is faith moving mountains, even though nobody sees the mountain move.

What the Disciples Missed

Jesus said, "If you have faith as small as a mustard seed..." We always focus on the seed. We forget He did not say, "If you have faith as STRONG as a mountain." He said small. Tiny. The smallest seed. Because the point was never the size of the faith. The point was the direction of it. Even a seed, planted, grows.

Your small faithfulness – the one verse, the one breath, the one honest sentence "God, I am struggling" – is a seed. It is planted in the soil of your suffering. And it grows. Not on your timetable. Not in the way you expect. But it grows.

The Permission You Need

I want to give you permission today, and I say this not as a preacher but as someone who has crawled more than he has walked: you do not have to perform your faith for anyone. You do not need to pray for an hour to be spiritual. You do not need to read a chapter a day to be faithful. You do not need to have answers when people ask how you are.

One verse. One breath. One sentence to God. That is enough. That has always been enough.

The poor soul – the soul I write about in this book – is not the soul that has failed. It is the soul that has been stripped bare. And in that bareness, it discovers that God was never impressed by the performance. God was always looking at the heart. And the heart that whispers "I am still here, Lord" is the heart God answers.

Your small faithfulness is not small to God. It is everything.
