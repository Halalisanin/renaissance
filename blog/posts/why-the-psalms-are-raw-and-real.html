Renaissance of the Poor Soul

On Scripture and Honesty

Why the Psalms Are Raw and Real

Halalisani Ngema March 2026

Originally published on renaissance.liviyo.co.za

Open your Bible to the Psalms. Not to the inspirational ones — not the ones printed on coffee mugs and greeting cards. Open it to Psalm 88. The one that ends in darkness. The one that does not resolve. The one where the psalmist cries out to God and God does not answer, and the poem ends with the word "darkness" as its final note. No resolution. No redemption arc. No gentle music swelling in the background. Just a man shouting into the dark, and the dark answering back.

That is the most honest book in the Bible.

I did not always see it that way. Growing up, the Psalms were the nice part of scripture — the part you read when you needed comfort, the part your grandmother quoted when the power was out and the candles were lit and the house felt close to God. And some of them are like that. Psalm 23 is a masterpiece of comfort. Psalm 91 is a fortress of protection. Psalm 139 is a love letter so tender it makes you ache.

But the Psalms are not only comfort. They are not only gentle. They are a full, unedited record of what it sounds like when a human being talks to God without filtering themselves first. And if you have never read them that way — if you have only read the curated version, the highlight reel — then you have missed the most important thing the Psalms have to offer.

David Did Not Edit Himself

David was a king. A warrior. A man after God's own heart — the Bible says that explicitly. And David wrote things in the Psalms that would make most church leaders uncomfortable. He said he was surrounded by death. He said his tears soaked his bed. He said he felt abandoned by God. He said he was so angry at his enemies that he wanted their children to be fatherless. He said he was weary of waiting. He said his soul was downcast.

"How long, Lord? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me? How long must I wrestle with my thoughts and day after day have sorrow in my heart?"

— Psalm 13:1–2

He did not say this once. He said it repeatedly, across dozens of psalms, in different seasons of his life. He said it when he was on the run from Saul. He said it after Bathsheba. He said it when his own son turned against him. The Psalms are not a book of a man who had it figured out. They are the journal of a man who kept showing up — broken, angry, confused, sometimes faithless — and refused to stop talking to God even when God seemed to have stopped talking back.

That is what makes them raw. Not the emotions themselves — we all have those. What makes them raw is that David refused to sanitise his prayers. He did not perform his faith. He lived it. Out loud. In writing. For anyone who would come after him to read.

Modern Faith Has Lost the Plot

I say this with love, and I say it as someone who has been inside the church my whole life. Modern faith — particularly the version of it that dominates social media and conference stages — has sanitised the Psalms beyond recognition. We have turned David's anguished cries into worship song lyrics with a major key backing track. We have taken his howling and turned it into a hook. We have taken his darkness and replaced it with a sunrise filter.

And in doing so, we have robbed people of the very thing the Psalms were meant to give them: permission.

Permission to not be okay. Permission to tell God the truth. Permission to say, "I am angry, and I do not understand, and I feel forgotten, and I am not sure I believe anymore." Permission to pray prayers that do not rhyme, that do not end with "in Jesus's name, amen" in a triumphant voice, that are not suitable for posting online.

The Psalms were never meant to be pretty. They were meant to be true. And truth, real truth, is not always photogenic.

The Connection to the Poor Soul

This is why the Psalms sit at the heart of Renaissance of the Poor Soul. Not as decoration. Not as proof that the author reads his Bible. As the backbone of the entire book. Because the Psalms and the poor soul share something essential: they both refuse to pretend.

A poor soul — the kind I describe in the book, the kind I have been — does not have the energy for pretence. When you have been emptied by life, when the reserves are gone, you do not have the capacity to package your pain into something palatable. You just have the pain. Raw. Unfiltered. Sometimes ugly. Sometimes desperate. Sometimes so raw that you are almost embarrassed by it, even though no one else is listening.

The Psalms validate that. They say: this is what faith sounds like when it is stripped of performance. This is what prayer looks like when you are not trying to impress anyone. This is what it means to be honest with God — not politely honest, not theologically honest, but actually honest. The kind of honest that makes people in the next room uncomfortable.

"I wrote this book the way David wrote the Psalms — not from a place of victory but from a place of honesty. And I believe that honesty, even when it is ugly, is closer to God than any polished prayer."

What the Psalms Taught Me About Prayer

Before I started writing this book, my prayers were careful. Polite. I would tell God what I was grateful for, mention a few requests, and close with something that sounded spiritual. It was fine. It was the kind of prayer that other people would nod at if they overheard it. But it was not real. It was the prayer of a man who was more concerned with sounding faithful than being honest.

The Psalms changed that. Psalm 88 changed that. The psalm that ends in darkness. The one that has no resolution. The one that just stops — mid-cry, mid-question, mid-darkness — and leaves you there. When I first read it as an adult, sitting in my car in a parking lot because I could not face going into work, something broke open in me. Not dramatically. Not like a dam bursting. More like a crack forming in a wall that had been holding back water for years.

I started praying like David prayed. Not beautifully. Not eloquently. Honestly. I started telling God things I had never said out loud. I started saying, "I am angry." "I do not understand." "I feel abandoned." "I am not sure I believe you are there." And the strange thing — the thing I did not expect — was that those prayers felt more like prayers than anything I had prayed before. They felt like they were reaching somewhere. Not because they were better constructed, but because they were true.

And truth, I have come to believe, is the only currency God accepts.

Giving the Reader Permission

If you are reading this and you have been carrying prayers that feel too raw for church, too angry for Bible study, too honest for your small group — the Psalms are for you. Not the curated ones. The raw ones. The ones where David says the things you have been thinking but were afraid to say.

You are not too broken for the Psalms. You are exactly who they were written for. The poor soul, the afflicted soul, the tormented soul — they are all in there. Every one of them. Crying out. Refusing to pretend. And in that refusal, finding something that the polished and the performed can never offer.

They found God. Not in the performance. In the honesty.

That is what the Psalms are. They are permission. Permission to be human before God. Permission to be raw. Permission to be real.

Take it. You need it more than you know.
