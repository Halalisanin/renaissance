Finding Rest When You Are Exhausted

September 1, 2026 · By Halalisani Ngema

There comes a point — and if you have lived long enough you know exactly what I mean — when you forget what peace feels like. Not the absence of trouble. Peace. That deep, settled, something-is-well-in-my-soul feeling. You forget it the way you forget a language you once spoke fluently. The words are still somewhere inside you, but you cannot form them anymore.

The weary soul. That is the soul I want to talk about today. Not the poor soul, not the lost soul, not the afflicted soul. The weary one. The one who has fought so long that fighting has become the only identity it knows.

The Mat You Have Been Carrying

There is a story in the Gospel of John, chapter five, that I return to again and again. A man has been lying beside the pool of Bethesda for thirty-eight years. Thirty-eight years. Think about that. Thirty-eight years of watching others get healed while he lay there, waiting for the water to move. Thirty-eight years of hope deferred, which, as the Proverbs say, makes the heart sick.

And then Jesus comes. And He asks a question that seems almost cruel in its simplicity: "Do you want to be made well?" The man does not say yes. He explains why he cannot be healed. The water moves too fast. Someone always gets there first. He has no one to carry him. He lists every obstacle, every reason, every years-old excuse.

And Jesus says something extraordinary. He does not explain theology. He does not ask for faith. He does not perform a long ritual. He says: "Stand up, take up your mat, and walk."

But here is what I want you to notice. Before the man could take up his mat, he had to put it down. Thirty-eight years of lying on that mat. It was not just something he carried — it was something he lived on. It was his bed, his home, his identity. When people asked, "Who is that man?" the answer was always, "The one on the mat by the pool." The mat was his story. His excuse. His sickness and his self. All rolled into one piece of woven material.

Jesus told him to take it up. But the first step — the real miracle — was putting it down.

"Rest is not the reward for being spiritual enough. Rest is the act of trusting that God can hold what you have been carrying."

Why We Cannot Put the Mat Down

I have been that man. Not by a pool for thirty-eight years, but by my own pool of exhaustion. Carrying the weight of a family. Carrying the weight of expectations — my own, my community's, my church's. Carrying the weight of being strong when I had nothing left. Carrying the mat of my own story, the one that said, "You must keep going. You cannot rest. Rest is for people who can afford it. Rest is for people who have done enough. You have not done enough."

The weary soul carries its exhaustion as a badge of honour. And nobody tells it to stop. In fact, the culture rewards it. "Hard worker." "Dedicated." "Strong." These words sound like praise. But sometimes they are chains.

I want to say something that might sound unspiritual: rest is not laziness. Rest is not giving up. Rest is not a sign that your faith is weak. Rest — real rest, soul rest — is one of the most radical acts of faith a person can perform. Because when you rest, you are saying to God: "I trust You enough to stop. I trust that the world will not fall apart if I sleep. I trust that You are still God even when I am doing nothing."

Sabbath and Teshuvah

In the book, I write about two Hebrew concepts that the Western church has largely forgotten. The first is Sabbath — not as a religious rule but as a spiritual rhythm. God rested on the seventh day. Not because He was tired. Because rest is built into the fabric of creation. The universe itself breathes: creation, rest, creation, rest. When we refuse to rest, we are not being productive — we are being unbiblical.

The second concept is teshuvah — which means "return." I will write more about it in another post, but here I want to connect it to rest. Teshuvah is the idea that you can always come back. That no matter how far you have wandered, no matter how deep the exhaustion, no matter how lost you feel — there is a path home. Rest is a form of teshuvah. When you lay down your burdens, you are returning to yourself. Returning to God. Returning to the person you were before the exhaustion took over.

The weary soul does not need another sermon about pushing through. It needs permission to stop. It needs someone to say, "You have fought enough. Lay it down. Not forever. Just for now. Just for today."

What It Feels Like to Rest

I will be honest with you. When I first allowed myself to rest, it felt like dying. Because the exhaustion had become so familiar that peace felt foreign. The silence was deafening. The stillness was terrifying. I did not know who I was without the fight.

But slowly — and I mean slowly, not in a moment, not in a sermon, not in a single prayer — slowly I began to feel something I had not felt in years. A quiet. A settling. Not the absence of problems, but the presence of something deeper than problems. A knowing, in my bones, that I was held. That I did not have to hold everything.

That is what rest feels like for the weary soul. Not a vacation. Not a break from responsibilities. A deep, cellular knowledge that you are not the one holding the world together. You never were. And the exhaustion you feel is partly the exhaustion of trying to do God's job.

The Invitation

If you are weary today — and I suspect many of you are, because weary people do not read blog posts about rest for entertainment — I want to invite you to something small. Not a pilgrimage. Not a retreat. Not a spiritual discipline. Just this: tonight, before you sleep, put something down.

It might be a worry. It might be an expectation. It might be the weight of someone else's opinion. It might be the belief that you have to be strong. Whatever it is, put it down. Not because the problem is solved. But because you are not the one who has to solve it.

Jesus told the man at the pool to take up his mat and walk. But the walking came after the laying down. Rest comes first. Always.

You have fought long enough. Put down the mat. You can pick it up again tomorrow if you need to. But tonight, rest.
