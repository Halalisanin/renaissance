The Pigeon That Taught Me About Resilience

By Halalisani Ngema · 2026-09-01

I saw it on a Tuesday afternoon, outside a petrol station in Mandeni. A pigeon — grey, ordinary, unremarkable in every way except one. It was missing a foot. The left leg ended just above where the toes should have been, a smooth, healed stump that told a story of something that had happened long ago and had long since stopped bleeding.

It hopped. It hopped from the pavement to the gutter, from the gutter to a patch of grass, and then — in a movement so fluid it almost looked deliberate — it launched itself into the air and flew. Not far. Not high. But it flew. It flew to a wall where two other pigeons were sitting, and it landed among them as if nothing had happened. As if the missing foot was a detail, not a destiny.

I stood there for a long time. I don't know why. Maybe because I saw myself in that pigeon. Maybe because I had been hopping for years — from one broken thing to the next, from one loss to the next, from one closed door to the next — and I had started to believe that the hopping was all there was. That I would never fly again.

But the pigeon flew. It did not wait for its foot to grow back. It did not sit in the gutter and mourn the foot. It did not compare itself to the other pigeons who had two feet and could walk properly. It simply used what it had — one foot, two wings, and an stubborn refusal to stay on the ground.

There is a word for this in the book of Hebrews: endurance. The writer speaks of runners who endure the race, not because the race is easy, but because they see the finish line. The pigeon did not know about finish lines. It only knew about the wall, and the other pigeons, and the need to be somewhere else.

If you are reading this and you feel like the pigeon — damaged, limping, unsure whether you can still fly — let me tell you what I learned that Tuesday afternoon. Your foot is not coming back. But your wings still work. The damage is real, but it is not the whole story. You can still get to the wall. You can still find your people. You can still fly.

Renaissance of the Poor Soul is, in many ways, a book about pigeons. About souls that have been hurt, broken, diminished — and that refuse to stop moving. It is not a book about healing in the way we usually mean it. It is a book about living with the wound and flying anyway.
