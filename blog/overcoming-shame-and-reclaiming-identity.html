Renaissance of the Poor Soul

On the Tormented Soul

Overcoming Shame and Reclaiming Identity

Halalisani Ngema March 2026

Originally published on renaissance.liviyo.co.za

There is a wound that is worse than guilt. Worse than failure. Worse than grief. It is the wound that attacks not what you have done but who you are. It does not say, "You made a mistake." It says, "You are the mistake." It does not say, "That was a bad choice." It says, "You are a bad person, and everyone who sees you can tell." That wound has a name. It is shame. And if you have ever lived inside it, you know that it is the loneliest place on earth.

I know shame. Not as a concept I studied in theology books. As a companion. As something that sat beside me in the car, stood behind me in the queue, lay next to me at night. Shame was the voice that told me not to go to church because people would know. The voice that told me not to answer the phone because there would be another bill, another demand, another reminder that I was failing. The voice that told me not to pray because God had already seen everything and there was nothing left to say.

In the book, I call this the tormented soul. Not afflicted – afflicted is different. Afflicted is what happens to you from the outside. Tormented is what happens on the inside. Tormented is when you become your own accuser. When the worst courtroom in your life is not a judge's bench but your own mind, and the gavel falls a hundred times a day.

The Difference Between Shame and Guilt

This distinction changed my life when I first encountered it, and I write about it in the book because I think it is one of the most important things a person can understand about their own suffering.

Guilt says: I did something wrong. Shame says: I am something wrong.

Guilt is painful, but it is useful. Guilt tells you that your behaviour crossed a line. It tells you that you hurt someone, that you broke something, that you need to make it right. Guilt points at a specific action. It says, "That thing – that is what needs to change." And because it is specific, it can be addressed. You can apologise. You can make amends. You can change the behaviour. The problem is identifiable, which means the solution is possible.

Shame does not work like that. Shame does not point at a behaviour. It points at you. It says, "You are the problem. Not what you did – you." And because you cannot change yourself the way you change a behaviour – you cannot apologise for your existence, you cannot make amends for being who you are – shame becomes a prison with no door. You are locked inside yourself, and there is no action you can take that feels sufficient to unlock it.

Shame accumulates. It does not arrive all at once. It builds. One failure adds a layer. A rejection adds another. A public humiliation – the kind where you can feel people looking at you, the kind where you want the ground to open and swallow you – adds another. And over time, these layers become so thick that you cannot see yourself through them. You cannot see the person underneath all the shame. You can only see the shame itself, and you assume that is who you are.

How Shame Accumulates

In my own life, shame accumulated in stages. The first failure – the business that did not work, the relationship that fell apart – brought guilt. I felt bad about what had happened. I knew I had made mistakes. I could name them. But when the second failure came, and the third, and the fourth, something shifted. The guilt did not go away, but something heavier settled on top of it. Something that did not distinguish between my actions and my identity.

Shame arrived quietly. It sounded like this: "Everyone can see." "Everyone knows." "You are the person everyone warns their children about." "You are the one who could not get it together." It was not rational. I knew, on some level, that not everyone was thinking about me. But shame does not operate on logic. It operates on feeling. And the feeling was total. It was suffocating. It was the feeling of being transparent – of walking through the world with your worst moments printed on your forehead for everyone to read.

The rejection made it worse. Not just romantic rejection, though that was part of it. The rejection of not being chosen. Not being called back. Not being the one they wanted. Every unanswered application, every polite "we have decided to go in a different direction," every phone call that did not come – each one added another layer to the shame. Each one whispered: you are not enough. You were never enough. And everyone is beginning to see it.

The loss made it worse still. The death of my mother. The death of my brother. Loss, when combined with shame, becomes a particular kind of poison. Because shame says that even your grief is suspect. That you do not deserve to mourn. That the people you lost are better off not being connected to someone like you. It is a lie. But when you are inside shame, lies sound like the only honest thing left.

"Shame is the one wound that makes you hide from the very people who could heal it. And that is its genius. That is its cruelty. It makes isolation feel like safety."

Remorse vs. Shame

I want to make this distinction because it matters practically, not just theoretically. Remorse is healthy. Remorse is what you feel when you recognise that your actions had consequences and you wish they had been different. Remorse leads to change. It is forward-looking. It says, "I want to do better, and I can." It is the soil in which growth happens.

Shame is not remorse. Shame does not lead to change. Shame leads to hiding. Shame says, "There is no point in trying to do better because you are fundamentally broken." Shame takes the energy that could be used for repair and redirects it into self-punishment. And self-punishment, no matter how sincere, does not produce growth. It produces paralysis.

The tormented soul in the book is the soul that has confused shame with remorse. It thinks that if it punishes itself long enough and hard enough, something will be redeemed. But redemption does not work through self-punishment. It never has. Redemption works through honesty, through connection, through the slow and painful process of letting another human being see you as you actually are – not as the polished version, not as the performance, but as the real, broken, struggling person underneath all the layers of shame.

Practical Steps for the Tormented Soul

I am not a therapist. I want to say that clearly. What follows is not clinical advice. It is what I have learned in my own journey, what I include in the book, and what I have seen work – not perfectly, not quickly, but genuinely – in the lives of people I have walked alongside.

1 Name the shame

You cannot fight what you cannot name. Shame thrives in vagueness – in the general feeling of being wrong without knowing why. The first step is to say, specifically, what you are ashamed of. Not "I am a failure." That is too broad. "I failed at this business, and I am ashamed because I told everyone it would work." Specific. Nameable. Addressable. When you name shame, you shrink it. Not to nothing. But enough to see around it.

2 Find one witness

Shame demands secrecy. It says, "If anyone knew the real you, they would leave." The antidote to secrecy is not confession in the formal, religious sense – though that can help. The antidote is witness. One person. One human being who will hear your story and not leave. Not someone who will fix you. Not someone who will quote scripture at you. Someone who will sit with you in it. Say, "I see you. That sounds heavy. I am not going anywhere." Finding that one person – even if it takes time, even if it is hard – is the most important thing you can do for your tormented soul.

3 Practice small faithfulnesses

Shame tells you that nothing you do matters, so why bother. The response to that lie is not grand gestures. It is small faithfulnesses. Making the bed. Showing up on time. Feeding someone. Writing one sentence. Walking to the end of the street and back. These are not solutions. They are rebuttals. Each small act of participation is a quiet declaration: I am still here. I still exist. I still matter enough to do this small thing. Over time, these small acts accumulate just like shame accumulated – but in the opposite direction. They build something. They build evidence against the lie.

4 Let faith be small

You do not need to believe everything to believe something. You do not need to have your theology sorted to pray. You do not need to feel God's presence to talk to God. The tormented soul often stops praying because it does not feel worthy. But worthiness is not a prerequisite for prayer. The Psalms are full of prayers from people who felt unworthy. David prayed from the depths of shame – after Bathsheba, after his son's rebellion, after his own failures. He did not wait until he felt clean to approach God. He approached God dirty. And that, I believe, is the only way any of us approach God. Not clean. Not worthy. Just honest.

You Are Not the Shame

I will end with this because it is the most important thing I have learned, and it is the beating heart of the book: you are not your shame. You are not the worst thing you have done. You are not the accumulation of your failures, your rejections, your losses. You are a soul – a real, specific, irreplaceable soul – that has been covered in layers of shame that do not belong to you.

The renaissance – the renewal, the coming back to life – begins when you start to peel those layers back. Not all at once. Not in a single dramatic moment. But slowly, honestly, with the help of at least one witness, in the company of small faithfulnesses, and with a God who is not afraid of your honesty.

You are not what shame says you are. You are what God says you are. And God says: you are mine.
